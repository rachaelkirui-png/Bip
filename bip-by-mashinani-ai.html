One free bip. The whole hill hears you.

Bip combines a free-callback voice line (IVR) with community radio, so any phone, even a kabambe, can report, ask and vote, and listening clubs decide together.

The problem in Nandi Hills

Three problems a farmer can't solve alone

Nandi Hills sub-county has four wards (Nandi Hills, Chepkunyuk, Ol'lessos and Kapchorua) and about 107,000 people. Tea grows on the slopes, and almost every farm also keeps maize and a dairy cow.

Yields slip and no one can say why

One farmer sees one field. The cause shows only when many farms are compared, and nobody is collecting the comparison.

With Bip: when 11 farms in Chepkunyuk describe the same yellow streaks, that is a pattern. A youth champion photographs it, and the extension officer names the cause on air.

The extension officer comes twice a year

Kenya has fewer than 5,000 public extension officers for over 8 million farmers. Questions wait months, and answers reach one farm at a time.

With Bip: one answer on radio reaches every club at once. Paid youth champions are the officer's eyes between visits, and a public board shows how long each question has waited.

The buyer names the price

Brokers paid below KSh 2,200 a bag of maize at harvest in 2026, when NCPB offered KSh 4,000. Milk hawkers pay about KSh 30 a litre against KSh 70 in town.

With Bip: farmers report what buyers offer, the radio reads the range back, and the clubs agree a floor price and sell together.

Problem statement

Because of Bip, a listening club in Chepkunyuk will agree a floor price and a selling date for its maize before brokers reach the village this season, which today each farmer bargains for alone at the gate with no reference price, and will get a named answer from the extension officer within 14 days, which today can take months. We know because brokers paid below KSh 2,200 a bag at harvest in 2026 while NCPB offered KSh 4,000, and Kenya has fewer than 5,000 public extension officers for over 8 million farmers.

How Bip works

Exactly what the small AI does, and what people decide

The AI sits in one box at the community radio station. It listens, sorts and counts. Every decision on this diagram is made by a person: the presenter, the clubs, the officer or the farmer.

    The AI does

    • Turn Kiswahili voice notes into text, on the Bip Box.
    • Sort each message into one of seven farming topics, or say it is not sure.
    • Count what is rising, where, since when, and which clubs have gone quiet.
    • Map where clubs agree and disagree, and keep minority reasons visible.
    • Fill fixed script templates and do the arithmetic behind the radio drama.

    The AI never does

    • Give farming advice. Advice comes from the extension officer, by name.
    • Guess when it is unsure. A person listens instead.
    • Set a price or pick the farmer of the month. Clubs decide those.
    • Put anything on air. The presenter approves every segment.
    • Need the internet, or anything on the farmer's phone.

      Listening clubs

      Six listening clubs across Nandi Hills

      Clubs are the unit of Bip, not the single farmer. They already exist as chamas, self-help groups and tea buying-centre groups. Each club listens together on Saturday, sends one group reporter to call in, and has its own youth champion.

      How a Saturday session runs

      6:45 PMThe club gathers at the chief's camp, a church or under a tree. Someone brings the radio.
      7:00 PMThey listen to Bip on Air together on the community station.
      7:37 PMDuring the baraza segment they argue it out, then vote together on one kabambe.
      8:00 PMThe group reporter bips once for the club and records what was decided.
      Next daysThe champion visits farms with problems and photographs the best plot for the showcase.

      Bip Champions

      Young people as the new extension workers

      Each club has a paid youth champion with a smartphone. Between the officer's visits, champions visit the farms that reported problems, take photos, check what has been tried, and showcase the club's best work on air. The officer reviews their photos in one sitting and answers on the radio.

      • 1Visit. The Bip Box gives each champion a weekly visit list from the reports, with the most urgent first. A visit happens only if the farmer said yes on the call.
      • 2Photograph and note. The phone camera works offline. Notes follow a short checklist: what, how many plants, since when, what was tried.
      • 3Sync at the station. Photos upload over the station's Wi-Fi on Saturday, or by 3G from the ridge when there is signal.
      • 4Officer review. The officer reviews a week of photo cases in one afternoon and records answers for the show.
      • 5Showcase. Each month a champion presents the club's best plot on air and on the club's photo board.
      • 6Translate and record. Champions translate Kalenjin voice notes, which builds the data for a Kalenjin model.

      Paid work, with a path

      Champions earn a stipend per verified visit, paid by M-Pesa. Places can be funded through NYOTA, the World Bank-financed youth programme that pays for on-the-job training; over 2,000 young people turned up for NYOTA verification in Nandi Hills constituency alone. After a season, champions can train as village-based advisers, a model AGRA used in Kiambu, where 115 advisers trained 16,000 farmers.

          The Bip Box

          No laptop to walk off with

          A laptop is portable, it has a screen and battery worth reselling, and it goes home with the presenter. So the small AI runs on a fanless mini-PC with no screen, locked in a steel cabinet in the station's transmitter room. If it is stolen anyway, the thief gets an encrypted brick, and the station is back to normal in two days.

          If the Bip Box is stolen

          1. Minute 0The thief gets nothing readable. The disk is encrypted and unlocks only with the station manager's PIN. Phone numbers are stored scrambled, recordings are deleted after 30 days, and names exist only where farmers agreed.
          2. Hour 1Keys and SIMs are cut off. Mashinani AI revokes the box's keys. Safaricom and Airtel block the two SIMs.
          3. SaturdayThe show still airs. The presenter runs a normal phone-in, and champions bring voice notes recorded on their phones.
          4. Day 1Same numbers back. Replacement SIMs keep the station's Bip numbers, so farmers notice nothing.
          5. Day 2Spare box installed. A spare kept at the sub-county agriculture office is set up from a USB image. The models are public files, and last night's encrypted backup restores the week's counts.

          The most that can be lost is one day of reports. Voice recordings are never backed up, on purpose.

          Network, satellites and cost

          Built for the weakest signal in the sub-county

          Bip uses the two layers that reach almost everyone: a voice call and FM radio. Data is a bonus for the station, never a requirement for farmers.

          Coverage by club

          Illustrative

          In use: OpenCelliD tower records, operator coverage maps, and a walk test by each champion, since a tower nearby doesn't guarantee signal in the hills. Serengonik's silence lines up with weak signal beyond the ridge, so the gap is about the network before it is about interest.

          Ways in

          • 1Bip (missed call)Works on Safaricom, Airtel and Telkom. The call never connects, so it costs the caller nothing.
          • 2Safaricom Reverse CallDial # before the station number; the station accepts and pays. Two requests a day are free, then KSh 0.50.
          • 3Club group callThe group reporter calls once for the whole club after the session.
          • 4Champion collectsWhere there is no signal, the champion records voice notes offline and syncs them at the station.

          What is in the cabinet

          • Bip Box: N100-class mini-PC, 16 GB RAM, encrypted SSD, about 6 W.
          • GSM gateway: two SIMs, Safaricom and Airtel, running the IVR with Asterisk, agreed with the operators and within Communications Authority rules. An operator voice API (Africa's Talking lists KSh 2.50 a minute outbound) is the fallback where internet is steady.
          • Power: UPS battery and a small solar panel, with surge protection.
          • Prompts: recorded in the presenter's own voice. People trust a voice they know.
          • Weekly sync: rain and price summaries of a few kilobytes, over 3G from the ridge, by USB from the county office, or by satellite internet where nothing else reaches (Starlink Residential Lite listed at KSh 4,000 a month in 2025).

          Satellite data, used weekly

          • CHIRPS rainfall: 5-day totals for each club's cell, beside the elders' indicators.
          • Sentinel-2 via Digital Earth Africa: crop condition, to check whether streaked-maize reports match greenness loss.
          • NASA POWER: daily temperature and rain by coordinates, so advice fits the weather that actually happened.

          Each arrives as a short summary, not imagery. Farmers never download anything.

          Evidence, data and guardrails

          Why this design, and why AI

          Each part below answers one of the judging questions.

          Does it meet the rules?

          • A device farmers already have. Any phone that can make a call, plus the household radio. About 27 million of Kenya's 80 million connected phones are still feature phones (CA, June 2026).
          • Core works offline. Calls ride the voice network through a GSM gateway, and the show rides FM. The models run on the Bip Box with no internet.
          • Small models. The topic model is a few KB and trains in milliseconds. Speech to text is side-loaded once by USB.
          • Local language. Farmers speak and hear Kiswahili. Kalenjin (Nandi), the home language for many here, is the less-supported case: today it goes to youth champions to translate, and their transcripts become training data for a Kalenjin model.

          Why AI, and not just SMS or a spreadsheet?

          • It has to listen. Many farmers won't type a long SMS, and a spreadsheet can't hear a voice note. Speech to text is what lets a voice count.
          • Volume. 150 two-minute voice notes is about five hours of audio a week. One presenter can't listen, sort and count that before Saturday.
          • Patterns and absences. The value is in the counts: what is rising, where, since when, and who has gone quiet.
          • Deliberation at radio scale. Mapping where 130 voters across six clubs agree and disagree, and keeping minority reasons visible.
          • Simple tools do the rest. SMS confirms, FM broadcasts, the menu is fixed. AI is used only where they fall short.

          The topic model, tested

          Synthetic test set

          A multinomial naive Bayes classifier over Kiswahili words and four-letter word pieces, which copes with Kiswahili's prefixes and suffixes. It chooses only from a fixed list of seven topics, including livestock and milk, which matters in Nandi.

          Fail-safe: below 55% confidence, or with fewer than two known farming words, it says it is not sure and hands the message to a person. When most words are not Kiswahili, it routes to a translator.

          What this test does not show: how it performs on real farmers' speech over 2G lines. The test sentences were written separately from the training sentences, by us. The pilot's first job is 200 real calls, transcribed and labelled by the champions.

          Responsible AI

          • A person makes the final call. The presenter approves every segment, club chairs send buyer messages, and clubs choose the farmer of the month. The AI never sets a price.
          • Fail-safe. Low confidence becomes "a person will listen". Thin data becomes "too early to call a trend" on air.
          • No invented advice. Topics, menus and segments are fixed lists. Farming advice comes only from the extension officer, by name.
          • Where data sits. On the Bip Box, encrypted, in the county. Recordings deleted after 30 days. Phone numbers stored scrambled. Registered under Kenya's Data Protection Act (2019).
          • Consent. Asked on every call: radio with name, without name, or not at all. Champion visits only on a yes. Elders credited only if they agree.
          • Lost or shared phone. Nothing is stored on the farmer's phone except one SMS.
          • Bias. The gaps panel tracks women, youth and silent clubs every week. Kalenjin callers reach a person, not a wrong guess.

          Scale, jobs and what happens next

          • Radio already reaches farmers here. 88% of tea farmers in the Nandi Hills programme area listen to local radio daily (Farmers' Voice Radio), and radio reaches 77% of rural Kenyans (CA, 2025).
          • Proven base. Participatory radio with listening groups and mobile interaction raised engagement and adoption across six African countries (Hudson et al., 2017).
          • Reuse. Kenya had 252 FM stations on air in 2024. Any county with a station and a voice network can run a Bip Box.
          • Youth jobs. Six champions per sub-county, plus translators and a hub operator, fundable through NYOTA placements.
          • Pilot plan. Co-design with two clubs first, so farmers shape it from the start. Then 200 real calls labelled, then eight weeks on air with one station.
          • What we will measure. Share of women callers, questions answered within 14 days, price reported against price received, and whether clubs reach and keep a floor price.

          My take · What localising AI means to me (draft for the video)

          Localising AI means the community owns the questions, not only the answers. My research found that farmers usually enter digital advisory at the end, testing tools someone else designed. Bip starts where decisions are already made in Nandi: the baraza, the chama and the radio. The AI listens, sorts and counts. The people decide. And it means paid work for young people here, as champions, translators and the builders of the Kalenjin data.
